Rōmaji

Ichiwa no karasu ga tobu…

Haiiro no kumo to

Hitotoki,

Notes

I translated korvo as カラス (karasu) rather than ワタリガラス (watarigarasu). While watarigarasu specifically means “raven,” Japanese poetry commonly uses karasu unless the distinction between raven and crow is important.
Laro becomes カモメ (kamome) (“seagull”).
Eliretas (“peeks out”) is rendered as 顔をのぞかせる (kao o nozokaseru), a natural poetic expression meaning “to peek out” or “to show one’s face.”
The final line, 静寂が訪れる (seijaku ga otozureru), literally “silence arrives,” is a natural Japanese poetic way to express “There is silence for a moment,” while preserving the stillness of your original.

Literal English Gloss

17 October 2011

A raven flies…
A seagull follows behind…

The sun
comes forth from clouds
gray and orange.

I stand
beside the road by a house.

For a little while,
there is silence.

Notes

cacalōtl is the Classical Nahuatl word for a raven or crow.
cālotl is used here for “seagull.” Since Classical Nahuatl speakers had no contact with true oceanic gulls in the European ornithological sense, this is an adapted choice rather than a historically exact one.
Tōnatiuh is both “the Sun” and the Sun deity; in Nahuatl poetry the celestial body and the divine name naturally overlap.
patlāni means “flies.”
quitoquilia means “follows, goes after.”
quiztiuh conveys “comes forth, emerges,” matching the nuance of your Esperanto eliretas.
Rather than a literal “there is silence,” I used ca oncān moyōlcehui in tlatzīntli, “there, quietness settles,” which echoes the calm, contemplative tone found in Classical Nahuatl poetic style.

Because Classical Nahuatl poetry often favors parallelism and evocative imagery over direct statement, this version reads as something that could plausibly have been composed in a Nahua literary register while remaining faithful to your original scene.

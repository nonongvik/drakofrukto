🌸 1. The  -meh plural (very common)
Used for many nouns, especially those ending in -tl, -tli, or certain consonants.
Pattern:
	•	Drop -tl / -tli
	•	Add -meh
Examples:
	•	xōchitl → xōchimeh (“flower → flowers”)
	•	calli → calmeh (“house → houses”) (note contraction)
	•	tochtli → tochtmeh (“rabbit → rabbits”)
👉 This is probably the most recognizable plural pattern.

🌿 2. The -tin plural
Another very common plural, often used with:
	•	people
	•	animals
	•	some objects
Pattern:
	•	Keep the stem
	•	Add -tin
Examples:
	•	pipiltin (“children, nobles”) ← pilli
	•	māzātl → māzātin (“deer”)
	•	coyōtl → coyōtin (“coyote”)
👉 Sometimes both -meh and -tin can exist for similar nouns, but usage can be lexical (you just learn which one fits).

🌺 3. Reduplication (very Nahuatl-like)
Some nouns form plurals by reduplicating the first syllable, often along with a suffix.
Pattern:
	•	Repeat first syllable (usually shortened)
	•	Add plural ending (often -meh or -tin)
Examples:
	•	tōchtli → totochtin (“rabbit → rabbits”)
	•	coyōtl → cocoyōtin (“coyote → coyotes”)
👉 This gives Nahuatl its rhythmic, poetic feel.

🌼 4. Irregular / lexical plurals
Some words have forms you just memorize.
Examples:
	•	tlācatl → tlācah (“man → men”)
	•	cihuātl → cihuāmeh (“woman → women”)
👉 These often reflect older sound changes.

Nahuatl often doesn’t mark plural at all when:
	•	a number is present
	•	plurality is obvious from context
Examples:
	•	ce xōchitl = one flower
	•	ōme xōchitl = two flowers
	•	miyac xōchitl = many flowers
👉 This is completely natural—not “missing grammar.”

🌷 6. Animate vs. inanimate tendency
Plural marking is more common with animate nouns (people, animals).
Inanimate nouns are often left unmarked unless emphasis is needed.

In Classical Nahuatl, nouns can serve directly as predicates (no copula like “to be” is required). So you get structures like:
	•	xōchitl → “it is a flower / they are flowers”
Number is not obligatorily marked on the predicate noun. Instead, it’s inferred from:
	•	the subject (if expressed)
	•	context
	•	or sometimes plural marking elsewhere

With explicit subject
	•	in xōchitl → “it is a flower”
	•	in xōchimeh → “they are flowers” (explicit plural)
	•	in xōchitl (with plural subject understood) → “they are flowers” ✔ still possible

Key idea
Nahuatl is comfortable leaving number underspecified unless there’s a reason to emphasize it. So:
	•	xōchitl = “flower / flowers” (as a predicate, number-neutral)
	•	xōchimeh = “flowers” (explicit plural, often for clarity or emphasis)

	•	English “fish” (singular or plural)

	•	xōchitl does not force singular—it’s number-flexible
	•	If you need to stress plurality, you’d use xōchimeh

🌸 1. The absolutive suffix
Most standalone nouns in Classical Nahuatl carry an ending called the absolutive:
	•	-tl → xōchi-tl (“flower”)
	•	-tli → toch-tli (“rabbit”)
	•	-li → cal-li (“house”)

👉 This ending basically means:
“this noun is standing on its own (not possessed, not compounded)”

🌿 2. The key rule: absolutive
drops
in certain contexts
The absolutive disappears when the noun is no longer independent.

(a) In possession
When something is possessed, the absolutive is removed:
	•	xōchitl → “flower”
	•	no-xōchiuh → “my flower”
	•	mo-xōchiuh → “your flower”

👉 Notice:
	•	-tl is gone
	•	possession prefixes appear (no-, mo-, etc.)

(b) In compounds
Same thing—first element loses the absolutive:
	•	xōchitl + calli → xōchi-calli → “flower-house” (e.g., a garden building)

👉 The noun becomes a stem, not a standalone word.

(c) Before certain suffixes
If you add derivational endings, the absolutive often drops:
	•	xōchitl → xōchi- + suffix

When you add a possessor, the noun is no longer “free”—it’s dependent on another element.
Structure:
[Possessor prefix] + [STEM] (+ possessive ending)

	•	xōchi-tl → no-xōchi-uh (“my flower”)

	1.	-tl drops → the noun is no longer independent
	2.	A prefix anchors it (no- “my”)
	3.	A new ending (-uh) stabilizes the possessed form
👉 So:
	•	-tl = independence
	•	no-…-uh = dependence (bound to a possessor)

	1.	xōchi-tl → xōchi- (drop absolutive)
	2.	That stem attaches to another noun
	3.	Only the final element keeps its absolutive

In compounds, only the last noun is “closed” (absolutive)

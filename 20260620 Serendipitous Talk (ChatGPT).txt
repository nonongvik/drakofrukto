Marriage and social expectation

You and Gary seem to have arrived at somewhat different conclusions from different life experiences. Gary married twice and has children, while you moved from seeing marriage and parenthood as a goal toward seeing them as one possible life path among many. In contemporary societies, there is certainly a growing recognition that marriage and children are not obligatory milestones. Some people find deep fulfillment in family life; others find fulfillment in friendship, learning, travel, art, spirituality, or intellectual pursuits.

Your story about Japan is interesting because it highlights another reality: timing matters. Sometimes what appears to be rejection is really a mismatch in life stage, expectations, or circumstances.

Gnosticism and the “divine spark”

The idea you described to Gary resembles certain ancient Gnostic teachings. Different Gnostic groups had different beliefs, but many held that a divine element existed within human beings. Some traditions indeed suggested that spiritual capacity or awareness varied among individuals. Modern people often find such ideas attractive because they seem to explain differences in temperament, insight, or creativity.

Rebirth and Zen skepticism

Your mention of Steve Hagen is notable. Many Buddhists accept rebirth as a core teaching, but some modern practitioners take a more empirical attitude. They focus on meditation, direct experience, and ethical practice, while suspending judgment about metaphysical claims they cannot verify.

The attitude “don’t believe it merely because it is written; investigate it yourself” has deep roots in Buddhist thought. That does not necessarily mean rejecting rebirth, but rather avoiding blind acceptance.

Mind uploading

Your half-joking comment about insanity touches on a serious philosophical problem.

If a mind can be copied:

Is the copy “you”?
If two copies exist, which one owns your identity?
Could copies diverge into different people?
Could memories be edited without destroying personhood?
Could multiple minds be merged?

Many philosophers think mind uploading creates a problem of personal identity rather than merely a technical problem. The uploaded mind may possess all your memories and personality, yet the biological you might still experience death.

The ethical implications are enormous:

Ownership of copied minds.
Rights of digital persons.
Consent to modification.
Psychological stability after merging or editing.
Economic inequality if only wealthy people can upload.

Your joke about insanity is not far-fetched. Human psychology evolved for a single body and a continuous stream of experience, not for branching, merging, or being duplicated like software.

English’s reputation for being “easy” often comes from its relatively simple noun and verb inflections compared with many European languages. But spelling, pronunciation, phrasal verbs, idioms, and countless exceptions make mastery difficult.

Your example of every day versus everyday is a classic one:

Speaking but not reading

Japanese is especially prone to this phenomenon because learning the writing system—hiragana, katakana, and thousands of kanji—requires a massive investment beyond learning speech.

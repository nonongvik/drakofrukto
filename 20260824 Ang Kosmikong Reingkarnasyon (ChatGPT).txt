VIK: 2026-08-26. There is a difference between Buddhist “rebirth” via an “ever-changing stream of consciousness” and Hindu “reincarnation” via a “soul,” correct?

CHATGPT:

Yes—that is a major distinction, although both traditions use overlapping English vocabulary, which can make them sound more similar than they actually are.

Buddhism: rebirth without a permanent soul

In most Buddhist traditions, there is no permanent, unchanging self or soul (ātman) that travels from one body to another. This follows from anattā/anātman, “not-self.”

Instead, rebirth is understood in terms of a causal continuity or stream of processes:

life A → death → karmic continuity → life B

But the person in life B is neither completely identical to nor completely unrelated to the person in life A.

A classic analogy is a flame lighting another candle. The second flame comes from the first causally, but it isn’t literally the same flame.

So your phrase “ever-changing stream of consciousness” captures something important, although “stream of consciousness” can be slightly misleading if it sounds like Buddhism posits a single substance called consciousness that persists. Buddhist consciousness itself is also conditioned, impermanent, and changing.

Hindu traditions: ātman and reincarnation

Many Hindu traditions, by contrast, affirm some form of ātman—a deeper self that persists through changing bodies.

The familiar model is:

ātman → body A → death → body B → death → body C → …

The body changes, but the underlying self continues.

The Bhagavad Gītā famously compares changing bodies to changing clothes: the embodied self discards an old body and takes on another.

There is an important complication, though: “Hinduism” isn’t philosophically uniform. Different schools understand the relationship between ātman, individual identity, Brahman, karma, and rebirth differently. Advaita Vedānta, for example, ultimately identifies ātman with Brahman, whereas other Vedānta schools maintain different relationships between the individual self and ultimate reality.

The really interesting contrast

You could put the distinction like this:

	| Buddhism	| Many Hindu traditions
Permanent soul/self? | No — anattā | Yes — ātman, in many schools
What continues? | Causal continuity of conditioned processes | The enduring self/ātman
Rebirth | Rebirth | Reincarnation
Identity across lives | Neither exactly the same nor completely different | A continuing self inhabits different bodies
Ultimate goal | Nirvāṇa | Mokṣa, though its meaning varies by school

So there’s a fascinating paradox:

Hindu reincarnation: “Something enduring moves through different lives.”

Buddhist rebirth: “There is continuity between lives, but nothing permanent has to move between them.”

And this makes your earlier Cluster thought experiment even more interesting. A Hindu-style interpretation could ask, “Which body will my soul inhabit next?” A Buddhist interpretation would ask something more like, “What conditions will arise from this karmic process next?”

The latter doesn’t necessarily require a cosmic traveler moving from Earth to an alien planet. The continuity itself is what matters. That is a very different metaphysical picture from the conventional Western idea of a soul jumping from body to body.

CHATGPT:

Yes—your intuition is quite good, especially because you are distinguishing different dimensions of “hardness” rather than treating a language as intrinsically hard or soft.

I would treat hardness/stiffness ↔ softness/flow as a phonetic-perceptual continuum, not as a linguistic property with a single objective measurement.

1. Word endings: one of the strongest cues
Your Spanish/Mandarin versus English/Cantonese contrast captures something real.

Languages can have very different phonotactic profiles:

	•	Spanish strongly favors syllables ending in vowels, /s/, or sonorants. Words such as casa, amigo, grande, español tend to produce an open, continuous sound.
	•	Mandarin has relatively simple syllable structure and restricts codas largely to nasals /n ŋ/ (with /ɻ/ in some analyses/dialects).
	•	English permits a much larger inventory of codas and consonant clusters: texts, asked, strengths, world, sixths.
	•	Cantonese has historically preserved a particularly salient set of stop codas /p t k/ (although their phonetic realization varies), giving many syllables a perceptible “closure.”

So you can get an auditory impression something like:

open → resonant → flowing → soft
CV, CVN, CVs

versus

closed → interrupted → articulated → stiff
CVT, CVK, CCVC, CVCC, CCVCCC

This is partly why English can sound more “angular” than Spanish even though English vowels themselves can sound comparatively less sharply differentiated.

2. Vowels create a completely different kind of hardness
This is the fascinating part of your observation.
You can have a language whose consonants are soft but whose vowels are very sharply delineated.
Spanish and Esperanto are good examples for your purposes. Their relatively stable vowel systems make:

a e i o u

sound like five fairly definite targets.

English, by comparison, has a much more complicated vowel system, including diphthongs, reduced vowels, and extensive unstressed-vowel reduction. To an outsider, English vowels can therefore seem to slide, compress, centralize, or melt into one another.

So there are actually two different things we might call hardness:

Articulatory/segmental hardness

“How sharply does each sound terminate or begin?”

Vocalic/target hardness

“How clearly does each vowel occupy its own acoustic space?”

And they can point in opposite directions.
You could therefore get:

Language | Consonantal impression | Vowel impression

Spanish | relatively smooth | clear/distinct
Esperanto | relatively smooth | clear/distinct
Mandarin | relatively smooth | fairly distinct
English | clustered/abrupt | variable/reduced
Cantonese | closed/abrupt in many syllables | relatively distinct

 That’s why saying simply “English is harder” or “Spanish is softer” loses something important.

3. Consonant quality matters too

It’s not only whether a language permits clusters.
Compare:

m n ŋ l r j w

with

p t k b d g

The first group consists largely of sounds that can continue indefinitely or transition smoothly. The second contains complete closures.

That gives another continuum:

approximants → nasals → fricatives → affricates → stops

roughly moving from more continuous to more interruptive articulation.

And even fricatives differ. /s/ has a very crisp, sharp acoustic character; /ʃ/ is often perceived as softer; /f/ can sound relatively diffuse.

So a language rich in /m n l w j/ can acquire a very different “texture” from one rich in /k t p s ʃ/.

4. Stress and reduction can make a language sound soft

This is where English becomes particularly interesting.

English has strong stress-based reduction:

photograph → photography
can → often /kən/
and → often /ən/ or /n/

Unstressed vowels can become schwa or otherwise weaken.

That creates a kind of phonetic elasticity: sounds aren’t necessarily pronounced as maximally distinct dictionary forms in connected speech.

Spanish tends to preserve vowel quality much more consistently:

para doesn’t normally become something equivalent to English p’r.

So English can simultaneously be:
	•	hard/stiff in its consonant structure
	•	soft/mushy in its unstressed vowels

That apparent contradiction is exactly why your concept is useful.

5. Rhythm contributes enormously
I’d add rhythm as another axis.

English is often characterized as strongly stress-timed, while Spanish is more often described as syllable-timed (with the usual caveat that real languages don’t fall into perfectly discrete timing categories).

A strongly stress-prominent language can produce:

STRONG — weak — weak — STRONG — weak

with substantial reduction between stresses.

That can feel elastic, compressed, and flowing.
A language with more evenly salient syllables can feel:

TA-TA-TA-TA-TA

which paradoxically can sound more mechanically precise.

Thus your “soft English / hard Spanish vowels” observation makes perfect sense: English can sound soft at the prosodic level while being hard at the phonotactic level.

6. And then there’s perceptual familiarity

This is perhaps the biggest complication.
What sounds “soft” to a person is partly determined by what their own phonological system considers normal.

A Japanese speaker encountering an English cluster such as:

strengths

may perceive an enormous amount of consonantal hardness.

An English speaker hearing a language with unfamiliar phonemic contrasts might instead perceive that language as harsh—even if its syllables are mostly CV.

And for a learner, precision can itself feel hard.

That’s particularly relevant to your experience with Esperanto and Interlingua. Esperanto’s vowels are relatively stable and its phoneme-to-letter correspondence is unusually transparent. That can make pronunciation feel rigidly well-defined:

a must be /a/,
e /e/,
i /i/,
o /o/,
u /u/.

English gives the speaker much more phonetic latitude, but that latitude creates a different difficulty: you have to learn when the vowel changes, reduces, merges, or becomes part of a diphthong.

So:

“Clearly defined” ≠ “easy.”
“Variable” ≠ “soft” in every sense.

A multidimensional “language hardness” model

If we wanted to turn your intuition into something more systematic, I’d divide hardness/softness into at least six independent axes:

	1.	Coda hardness — how frequently syllables terminate in stops/clusters.
	2.	Cluster density — how many consonants can accumulate.
	3.	Consonant interruptiveness — prevalence of stops/affricates versus sonorants.
	4.	Vowel definition — how stable and acoustically distinct vowel targets are.
	5.	Reduction — how much vowels and consonants weaken in unstressed/connected speech.
	6.	Prosodic elasticity — how much stress, rhythm, tone, length, and intonation reshape the acoustic stream.

Then a language doesn’t get a single score. It gets a phonetic texture.

For example, your intuition could characterize Spanish approximately as:

soft consonantal texture + hard/clear vowels + relatively low reduction + flowing syllabic rhythm

and English as:

hard consonantal texture + soft/variable vowels + high reduction + highly elastic stress rhythm

while Cantonese might be:

harder syllable closure + relatively clear vowel nuclei + substantial tonal/prosodic specification

and Esperanto:

smooth syllable structure + unusually explicit vowel targets + comparatively little reduction + strongly segmentable phonology.

That last distinction is especially interesting:

Esperanto can sound “soft” in its consonant flow while simultaneously sounding “hard” because every phoneme seems to stand there in full daylight.

So I think your basic formulation could be sharpened to:

Languages aren’t simply hard or soft. They have different kinds of phonetic hardness. A language may be hard in its consonants, soft in its vowels, hard in its vowel contrasts, soft in its reductions, or stiff in its syllable boundaries. What we perceive as the overall “texture” emerges from the combination.

And that explains why two listeners can hear the same language and come away with almost opposite impressions of its “hardness.”

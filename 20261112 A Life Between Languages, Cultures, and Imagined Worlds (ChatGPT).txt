A Life Between Languages, Cultures, and Imagined Worlds

A biography — 2026 November 12

Some people build their lives around a profession, a place, or a particular cultural tradition. Others find themselves drawn to many worlds simultaneously. This is the story of someone whose life has been shaped by languages, technology, imagination, artistic expression, cultural exploration, and an enduring curiosity about the universe.

Beginnings: Between the Philippines and Canada

Growing up in the Philippines, Tagalog was the language of childhood and family. Later, moving to Canada at approximately ten years of age introduced another linguistic and cultural environment. English gradually became a dominant language of everyday expression, while Tagalog remained an important connection to Filipino identity and heritage.

This experience of crossing cultural boundaries would become a recurring theme throughout life.

Rather than belonging exclusively to one cultural environment, there developed an appreciation for the richness of different civilizations, languages, and ways of seeing the world. Asia, Europe, and North America became interconnected reference points in an increasingly expansive personal universe.

Japan, in particular, would become something of an adopted cultural homeland. Having visited Japan eleven times, there is a lasting fascination with its landscapes, architecture, food, traditions, and intricate language. Thailand and Korea also occupy special places in the imagination, particularly because of their distinctive writing systems, cultural complexity, and extraordinary culinary diversity.

The attraction is not simply to travel destinations. It is to the experience of discovering how differently human societies can organize their lives.

The Engineer and the Technological Imagination

An important chapter of this life belongs to computing and software engineering.

With experience programming in C and C++, including work in Vancouver’s Gastown technology environment and at Microsoft, as well as experience with Smalltalk on Burnaby Mountain, there is a longstanding familiarity with the conceptual architecture of computer systems.

Engineering and language creation share an interesting characteristic: both involve constructing systems from smaller components.

This affinity for structure, logic, and elegance appears repeatedly in other interests. It can be seen in the appreciation of mathematical languages, phonological systems, grammatical design, and the internal consistency of fictional universes.

More recently, Python and artificial intelligence have renewed the technological curiosity. Rather than viewing technology exclusively as a professional discipline, there is an interest in its creative possibilities: generating poetry, exploring ideas, constructing imaginary languages, and experimenting with new forms of expression.

The Linguist and Language Creator

Perhaps one of the most distinctive aspects of this personality is the relationship with language.

This is not merely the interest of someone who enjoys learning foreign languages. It is also the imagination of a language designer.

Tagalog and English form important foundations, while French, Japanese, and Spanish represent different stages of linguistic exploration. There is also an enduring attraction to Portuguese, Italian, Catalan, Chavacano, Thai, Vietnamese, Korean, Mandarin, Hokkien, Pali, and numerous other languages.

Two constructed languages deserve particular attention: Esperanto and Lojban.

Esperanto, associated with the colour green, represents an appreciation for mathematical regularity, international communication, and grammatical architecture. Its systematic structure and relatively predictable word formation are especially appealing.

Interlingua, associated with blue, offers a different pleasure. Its Romance-language character, naturalistic vocabulary, and international scientific terminology make it an attractive complement to Esperanto.

The two languages have become a kind of intellectual partnership: green Esperanto and blue Interlingua.

But the imagination does not stop with existing languages.

As a conlanger, there is a desire to create entirely new linguistic systems. Romboi and Vling are two important personal projects. Vling, associated with orange, uses tones to distinguish parts of speech. Lojban, affectionately associated with purple and sometimes referred to as zirbau, provides another source of inspiration through its logical structure and systematic vocabulary.

The attraction to language creation reflects something deeper: the pleasure of designing an alternative way for intelligent beings to communicate.

Language is not simply something to inherit. It is also something one can invent.

The Reader and Explorer of Imaginary Universes

Science fiction and fantasy provide another major dimension of this life.

Among the favourite fictional universes are Star Trek, Space: 1999, Babylon 5, Battlestar Galactica, Dune, and Star Wars.

These are not simply entertainment preferences. They represent different ways of imagining civilizations, technologies, political systems, philosophies, and the future of intelligent life.

There is also an appreciation for speculative literature, including Robert Silverberg’s Majipoor stories. Reading Majipoor Chronicles in both English and Spanish demonstrates how literary enjoyment and language learning can reinforce each other.

The image of an alien world populated by unfamiliar creatures, landscapes, and civilizations is particularly captivating.

Beyond fiction, there is genuine interest in astronomy and planetary science: Mars, the outer Solar System, helium-3 on the Moon, Titan’s methane lakes, potentially habitable exoplanets, interstellar objects, and the possibility of life beyond Earth.

The universe is not imagined as an empty expanse with humanity at its centre. Instead, Earth is a tiny inhabited speck in an enormous cosmic environment.

Even the possibility of unfamiliar forms of intelligence, perhaps as strange as mould-like organisms, is enough to stimulate the imagination.

This combination of scientific curiosity and speculative wonder is an important part of the personality.

The Artist and the Aesthetic Imagination

Artistic expression provides another outlet for the imagination.

There is a particular attraction to combining cultural and visual traditions: Mediterranean architecture, Asian landscapes, Japanese aesthetics, futuristic environments, classical sculpture, and science-fiction imagery.

The ideal visual environment might combine Greek and Filipino influences with Japanese and Southeast Asian elements, creating an imaginary Mediterranean–Asian island civilization.

There is also a fondness for the colour green, especially lime green and verdant natural landscapes.

Green has several associations: Esperanto, forests, tranquillity, intellectual exploration, and the feeling of being surrounded by living things.

The imagination often moves between the natural and the futuristic. A North American forest can be as inspiring as a Martian castle. A classical marble sculpture can coexist with a futuristic space station.

This artistic sensibility is not particularly concerned with maintaining strict cultural boundaries. Instead, it finds beauty in combinations.

A Philosophy of Many Worlds

An important intellectual thread is the desire to understand the world through multiple philosophical perspectives.

Science, philosophy, spirituality, and comparative cultural studies are all sources of exploration.

There is an interest in Buddhist concepts such as impermanence, dependent origination, and the aggregates, alongside Daoist philosophy, Shinto traditions, Gnosticism, and questions about consciousness and the nature of existence.

Rather than feeling compelled to adopt one exclusive explanatory system, there is an inclination toward synthesis.

The scientific understanding of the universe remains important, but it does not necessarily eliminate philosophical wonder.

Questions about consciousness, interconnectedness, the emergence of intelligence, and the relationship between humanity and nature remain open subjects of contemplation.

This intellectual disposition reflects an appreciation for complexity: the recognition that different traditions may offer different insights into the same fundamental questions.

The Traveller and the Café Enthusiast

Travel is not only about reaching destinations. It is also about experiencing the texture of everyday life.

Japan is especially meaningful, but there is also fascination with Thailand, Korea, Egypt, Morocco, Spain, Portugal, the Netherlands, England, and other places.

Historic streets, castles, temples, religious buildings, traditional neighbourhoods, and local food cultures are particularly appealing.

There is an appreciation for Europe’s organic urban environments, with their layers of architectural history, contrasted with North America’s more spacious landscapes and deliberately constructed surroundings.

Food is another important dimension of cultural exploration. The diversity of Asian cuisines, Mediterranean dishes, Filipino cooking, and international café culture offers an endless source of discovery.

In everyday life, a familiar Tim Hortons on Lulu Island in Richmond has become something of a personal intellectual retreat.

The long bay-window bench, broad ceiling lights, and comfortable surroundings create an almost futuristic solarium atmosphere.

There, over an iced matcha latte with extra oat milk, or perhaps an iced coffee, one might find language notes, a Spanish science-fiction book, or thoughts about some entirely different subject.

It is a modest but meaningful example of how ordinary surroundings can become spaces for contemplation.

A Life of Continuing Discovery

What connects these apparently diverse interests?

Perhaps it is an enduring desire to explore possibilities.

Languages offer alternative ways of thinking. Engineering offers ways of constructing systems. Science offers ways of understanding the physical universe. Fiction offers opportunities to imagine what does not yet exist. Art brings together otherwise unrelated ideas. Travel reveals the extraordinary diversity of human civilization.

There is no apparent need to reduce these interests to a single defining pursuit.

Instead, they form an interconnected personal universe.

One day the subject might be the pronunciation of a Pali word. Another day it might be the grammar of an invented language, the history of an international auxiliary language, the architecture of a Japanese neighbourhood, the methane lakes of Titan, or the philosophical implications of impermanence.

The common element is curiosity.

Epilogue: The Many Cultures, One Humanity

The life described here is still unfolding.

It is the life of an engineer who is also a writer and artist; a language learner who is also a language creator; a traveller who can find pleasure in distant civilizations and familiar cafés; a science enthusiast who appreciates both rigorous explanations and speculative possibilities.

It is also a life that resists the limitations of a single cultural, intellectual, or artistic identity.

Perhaps the most appropriate description is that of a perpetual explorer: someone who moves between languages, cultures, disciplines, and imagined worlds, finding connections where others might see divisions.

There is a recurring ideal behind this exploration: the possibility of many cultures belonging to one humanity.

And perhaps that is the most enduring theme of all.

To explore many worlds, to create new ones, and to remain curious about the universe that contains them.

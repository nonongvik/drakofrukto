I. A person of many worlds

Viktor Nonong Medrano, affectionately known as Viktoro, is an engineer, writer, artist, language enthusiast, and creator of constructed languages. His intellectual and imaginative life brings together several cultural traditions, scientific curiosity, literary exploration, and a deeply personal philosophy.

Born into a family with Filipino Austronesian, Hokkien Chinese, and mixed Southern European roots, Viktor grew up in the Philippines speaking Tagalog. At approximately ten years old, he moved to Canada, where his cultural experiences expanded considerably. His upbringing in the Philippines and subsequent life in British Columbia gave him an unusual perspective: he is simultaneously familiar with Asian, European, and North American cultural environments.

He is particularly attracted to the intricate cultural traditions of Japan, Thailand, and Korea, while maintaining an enduring affection for the Philippines and its multilingual heritage.

He sometimes describes his cultural imagination through the expression Asyanong Latinado—an Asian identity with a Latinized cultural sensibility. His interests in Mediterranean architecture, Romance languages, Asian aesthetics, and Filipino history make this an especially evocative description.

Viktor's intellectual personality is not easily confined to one discipline. Engineering, linguistics, anthropology, astronomy, speculative fiction, philosophy, spirituality, music, and visual art all belong to his personal universe.

Viktor's professional background includes software engineering and programming in C and C++. He worked in Vancouver's Gastown technology environment and for Microsoft, among other software companies. He also encountered Smalltalk while studying and working around Burnaby Mountain.

His technical background is accompanied by an interest in contemporary artificial intelligence and Python programming.

Yet his analytical tendencies extend far beyond computers. He enjoys examining how languages work, how civilizations develop, how belief systems evolve, and how scientific discoveries might transform our understanding of existence.

His school years also left significant impressions.

III. The linguistic universe: a collector, learner, and creator of languages

Language is arguably one of Viktor's most enduring intellectual passions.
Tagalog is his native language, but his linguistic interests have taken him across several language families and writing systems.

His formal language education includes:
	•	French, studied during his Canadian schooling and later education.
	•	Japanese, studied for approximately one year at the University of British Columbia.
	•	Spanish, also studied for approximately one year at UBC.
	•	Esperanto, which he subsequently pursued with considerable dedication through language clubs and independent study.

He is particularly devoted to Esperanto and Interlingua.
For him, the two constructed international languages represent different approaches to linguistic design.

Esperanto is green, representing its mathematical regularity, structural elegance, and relatively predictable grammar.

Interlingua is blue, representing its naturalistic Romance-language character, international vocabulary, and affinity with scientific terminology.

His interest in Interlingua became especially active in September 2026. He appreciates its naturalistic pronunciation, including its /v/ and /z/ sounds, and its accessibility to speakers familiar with English and Romance languages.

However, Viktor is not simply a language learner. He is also a conlanger.

His two principal constructed-language contributions are:
	•	Brown Romboi – associated with the idea of chocolate, with its own distinctive linguistic character.
	•	Orange Vling – a language in which tones mark parts of speech.

He is attracted to linguistic architecture, particularly structural elegance and low homonymy. His appreciation of Lojban, which he sometimes calls zirbau (purple language), reflects this preference.

His wider linguistic interests include:
	•	Hokkien, one of his ancestral languages.

He has also entertained an imaginative language-learning project he calls the Orange Dream, involving Thai, Vietnamese, and Korean.

For Viktor, languages are not merely communication systems. They are alternative ways of organizing reality, expressing identity, and understanding humanity.

IV. The research library: languages, anthropology, and human civilizations

An especially important part of Viktor's intellectual life is his collection of research books.

These are not simply decorative volumes. They represent his fascination with the diversity of human language and the possibility of understanding cultures through their linguistic structures.

Among his particularly important reference works are:

1. Haida Syntax, Volumes I and II — John Enrico

A substantial linguistic study of the Haida language. These volumes are especially meaningful within Viktor's interest in Indigenous languages and the grammatical architecture of languages outside the familiar Indo-European world.

2. Australian Aboriginal Grammar — Barry Blake

An important reference for understanding the grammatical structures of Australian Aboriginal languages.

3. Australian Languages — Dixon

A complementary area of interest that broadens his appreciation of Australia's remarkable linguistic diversity.

4. Introduction to Classical Nahuatl, Revised Edition — J. Richard Andrews

A study of Classical Nahuatl, the language associated with the Aztec world. It connects his linguistic interests with pre-Columbian history, civilization, and anthropology.

His Japanese reference interests are represented by this Esperanto-language encyclopedia, a particularly appropriate meeting point of his Japanese and Esperanto passions.

Collectively, these books reveal a person who is interested not only in learning languages but also in documenting, understanding, and appreciating the linguistic diversity of humanity.

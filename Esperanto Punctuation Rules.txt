Esperanto Punctuation Rules
Viktoro Medrano

VIK: 2026-06-08. What are the recommendations in Esperanto for putting periods within or without quotations, of phrases and words, in the middle or end of sentences?

CHATGPT:

In Esperanto, the usual recommendation is logical punctuation (logika interpunkcio), not the traditional American-English convention. In other words, punctuation belongs where it logically belongs: either to the quoted material itself or to the surrounding sentence. This is broadly similar to British usage.  

1. Quoted word or phrase in the middle of a sentence

If you are merely mentioning a word or phrase, the comma or period of the surrounding sentence goes outside the quotation marks.

	•	La vorto “amiko” estas tre ofta.
	•	Mi ne ŝatas la esprimon “senprobleme”, ĉar ĝi ofte sonas tro memfide.

2. Quoted word or phrase at the end of a sentence

The period belongs to the sentence, not to the quoted word, so it is usually placed outside.

	•	Li uzis la vorton “interlingvistiko”.
	•	Ŝi diris nur “eble”.

Many Esperantists consider this the most logical form because the period was not part of the quoted material itself.

3. A complete quoted sentence

If the quoted text is itself a complete sentence, its own punctuation stays inside the quotation marks.

	•	Li diris: “Mi venos morgaŭ.”
	•	Ŝi demandis: “Ĉu vi komprenas?”

Here the period and question mark belong to the quoted utterance.

4. A quotation embedded in a larger sentence

When a complete quotation is interrupted by a reporting clause, commas usually remain part of the quotation.

	•	“Mi venos morgaŭ”, li diris.
	•	“Mi ne certas”, ŝi respondis.

Some authors vary slightly in practice, but this is common Esperanto usage.

5. Question marks and exclamation marks

These follow logic rather than typography.

If the quotation itself is a question:

	•	Li demandis: “Kie vi loĝas?”

If the surrounding sentence is the question:

	•	Ĉu li vere diris “mi ne zorgas”?

Notice that the question mark is outside because your sentence is the question, not the quoted words.

Likewise:

	•	Kiel vi povas nomi tion “solvo”!

The exclamation mark belongs to the surrounding sentence.

A concise rule

Many Esperantists would summarize it this way:

Punctuation goes inside quotation marks only when it belongs to the quoted material; otherwise it stays outside.

Thus:

	•	Li diris: “Saluton.”
	•	Li uzis la vorton “saluton”.
	•	Ĉu vi aŭdis la vorton “saluton”?
	•	Li demandis: “Ĉu vi fartas bone?”

That approach is generally regarded as the most consistent with Esperanto’s preference for regularity and logical structure.

CHATGPT:

Certainly. Scenario 4 is where Esperanto style becomes especially interesting because it often follows the logic of the quotation rather than imposing punctuation mechanically.

4. A quotation embedded in a larger sentence

When a direct quotation is followed or interrupted by a reporting clause (li diris, ŝi demandis, ili respondis, etc.), the quotation is not necessarily treated as a fully closed sentence.

Common pattern

	•	“Mi venos morgaŭ”, li diris.
	•	“Mi ne certas”, ŝi respondis.
	•	“Jes”, li konsentis.
	•	“Ne”, ŝi kontraŭis.

The comma separates the quotation from the reporting clause, much as in many European languages.

⸻

Longer quotations

If the quoted speech continues after the reporting clause:

	•	“Mi venos morgaŭ”, li diris, “kaj mi restos ĝis vendredo.”
	•	“Mi ne scias”, ŝi respondis, “ĉu tio sukcesos.”

The reporting clause acts almost like a parenthetical interruption.

⸻

When the quotation is a question

If the quoted utterance itself is a question, the question mark belongs to it.

	•	“Kie vi loĝas?” ŝi demandis.
	•	“Ĉu vi komprenas?” li demandis.

Notice that no extra comma is needed after the question mark.

⸻

When the quotation is an exclamation

Likewise:

	•	“Bonege!” ŝi kriis.
	•	“Atentu!” li avertis.

The exclamation mark already closes the quoted utterance.

⸻

Multi-sentence quotations

If the quotation contains several sentences, the final punctuation remains inside the quotation.

	•	“Mi venis hieraŭ. Mi restos ĝis lundo.” li diris.

Or more commonly with a colon introducing the quotation:

	•	Li diris: “Mi venis hieraŭ. Mi restos ĝis lundo.”

Many stylists prefer the second form because it is visually cleaner.

⸻

Esperanto’s preference for the colon

Compared with English, Esperanto writers often use a colon before a direct quotation:

	•	Li diris: “Mi venos morgaŭ.”
	•	Ŝi respondis: “Mi ankoraŭ ne scias.”
	•	Li demandis: “Kial vi malfruis?”

This avoids some of the comma-placement dilemmas that occur in English.

⸻

Mentioning vs. quoting

Compare:

	•	Li uzis la vorton “amiko”.
    (The word itself is being mentioned.)

with

	•	“Amiko”, li diris, “estas iu, je kiu oni povas fidi.”
    (The word is part of quoted speech.)

The first is a linguistic mention; the second is a direct quotation.

A practical editorial guideline

If you write Esperanto prose, a useful approach is:

	1.	Use a colon to introduce direct speech:
	•	Li diris: “Mi venos.”
	2.	If a reporting clause interrupts the speech, use commas:
	•	“Mi venos”, li diris, “sed ne hodiaŭ.”
	3.	Keep question marks and exclamation marks with the quoted speech when they belong to it:
	•	“Kial?” ŝi demandis.
	•	“Atentu!” li kriis.

This tends to be the clearest and most widely accepted style among contemporary Esperanto writers.
